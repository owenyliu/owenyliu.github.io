Agent Firewall: Your AI agent stops at the line

AGI Healthcare Hackathon, sponsored by Lenovo, 2026 · Upstream project by Guoxuan Xu et al. · My contribution: UI/UX summary, evaluation, and presentation

A personal health agent needs your labs, records, and insurance to help you — and that is exactly what an attacker wants it to leak. Agent Firewall is a hybrid firewall that keeps sensitive agent data on your device, reads it with a local model when you ask it to, and stops at the line before money, health data, or passwords move — explaining the worst case in plain words and letting you decide.

Evaluation caveat, as the project states it: the attack numbers use a scripted model that always falls for the attack, so they test the firewall's plumbing, not how often a real model gets tricked. The one attack that still gets out (A14) is a paraphrased leak through an allowed host, kept in the demo on purpose.

What I did

I ran the full system end to end, verified the evaluation above, built the hackathon presentation around its real phone-approval UI, and wrote the project's UI/UX summary (branch feature/ui-ux-summary) — a complete account of its four user-facing surfaces as one design system, with strengths, known UX gaps, and recommendations. This page is the short version.

One idea, four surfaces

Your AI agent stops at the line, and you decide what crosses it.
SurfaceWhat it isKey UX decision
macOS popupNative dialog at the moment Claude Code tries to open a sensitive file: title, facts, fixed worst-case sentence, labelled AI detail, ⚠ reasons.Deny is the default button; timing out also returns Deny.
Phone approvalThe same card on your phone, with a live auto-deny countdown and a vibration when a new card arrives. Idle state: “Nothing needs you right now.”Approve needs an 800 ms press-and-hold; Deny is one tap. Friction follows risk.
DashboardThe line drawn at full width: giant counts for On this device vs. Sent to the cloud, with Denied / Sensitive / Unreadable in the gate between them; a searchable audit list; a policy composer.Every number is a filter button. Rules can only tighten — there is no “allow” rule to switch a protection off.
Public siteHero, demo video framed by the same gate visual, a faithful popup mock, the five outcomes in prose, and proof figures with their caveats attached.“What it doesn't do yet” is a section, not a footnote.

One design language

The dashboard and the public site share the exact same colour tokens, so a colour means the same thing everywhere:

ColourTokenMeaning
Mint--local #62e4b4Stayed on this device
Blue--cloud #92ccffSent to the cloud
Red--denied #ff808bDenied / blocked
Amber--sensitive #ffcb5eSensitive data involved
Violet--unsure #cdaeffUnreadable or unsure

Risk wording follows a two-layer rule: the guaranteed worst-case sentence is fixed per action (“This data leaves your Mac and can't be taken back.” / “The money is gone and may not be recoverable.”), never written by a model. A local model may add one specific sentence, shown separately and labelled “AI-written detail, may be wrong”, and only if it passes filters that reject reassurance, invented numbers, and overstatement. No model makes the routing or approval decisions — those are deterministic, because the text being reviewed can be hostile. Both web surfaces build the DOM with textContent only, for the same reason: file names and commands on a card can be attacker-controlled.

Known gaps & my recommendations

The full summary lists these from the project's own limits document. In short: the phone page's plain-HTTP token needs its warning on the page itself, not only in the docs; “Keep on my Mac” silently truncates long files at 20,000 characters and that caveat should reach the result card, the way AI worst-case text is labelled; expiring cards (90 seconds) need a “decide later” re-prompt that keeps deny-by-default; the tighten-only rule model deserves a one-sentence explanation in the composer itself; and the dashboard header should show scan coverage honestly — checked, partly read, and not yet scanned.
